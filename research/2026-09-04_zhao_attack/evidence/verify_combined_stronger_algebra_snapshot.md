### H3: every element occurs at most three times

Suppose a bad A or B sequence contains four copies of g. We already know g is nonzero. Quotient by the order-five subgroup generated by g and remove the four copies. In A the remaining length is 17, so the quotient has a nonempty zero sum of length at most 9. In B the remaining length is 16, so the second displayed constant supplies one of length at most 10. Thus H3 holds for both endpoints and every subsequence of a bad sequence.

## 2. PROVED_HERE: the integral group ring reaches a second p-adic layer

Let p≥5 be prime, G=C_p^4, and put

    J = Σ_{g∈G} X^g.

For any sequence T=(g_1,...,g_N), define the integral group-ring product

    F_T = ∏_{i=1}^N (1−X^{g_i}).

Then:

1. If N≥5(p−1)+1, every coefficient of F_T is divisible by p².
2. If N=5(p−1), then

       F_T ≡ −p c(T) J  (mod p²),                         (2)

   where, for coordinates g_i∈F_p^4,

       c(T) = Σ_{t∈F_p^4} ∏_{i=1}^N <t,g_i>  ∈ F_p.       (3)

In particular all nonidentity group coefficients also satisfy (2). This is stronger information than retaining only the identity coefficient modulo p.

### Proof

In the integral group ring modulo p² put y_j=X^{e_j}−1. The relation X^{p e_j}=1 gives

    y_j^p = −Σ_{a=1}^{p−1} binom(p,a)y_j^a.                (4)

Every coefficient on the right is divisible by p, and a single application reduces total degree by at most p−1. A monomial with all four exponents below p has total degree at most 4(p−1). Consequently any monomial of total degree at least 5(p−1)+1 needs at least two applications of (4) in every reduction to this basis. Each reduction path is divisible by p². Since every factor 1−X^{g_i} has no constant term in the y variables, this proves the first assertion.

For N=5(p−1), only the homogeneous part of degree N can survive modulo p². As N is even, that part is

    P(y)=∏_i <g_i,y>.

To survive exactly one application of (4), a degree-N monomial must have exponent pattern

    (2(p−1),p−1,p−1,p−1)

or one of its four coordinate permutations. The reduction must use the a=1 term of (4), and hence contributes −p times y_1^{p−1}...y_4^{p−1}. All other monomials require at least two p factors. Thus F_T is −p times the sum of these four coefficients of P times the top monomial.

Over F_p, the top monomial is J, because

    (X^{e_j}−1)^{p−1} = Σ_{a=0}^{p−1}X^{a e_j}.

Finally sum P(t) over F_p^4. A monomial contributes zero if any exponent is zero, or if any positive exponent is not divisible by p−1. Each of the four surviving degree-N patterns contributes the factor (−1)^4=1. Therefore its sum is precisely the coefficient sum just obtained, proving (2) and (3). This proof allows repeated or zero elements and does not require the bad-sequence assumption.

Because P(at)=P(t) for a≠0, one can also write

    c(T)=−Σ_{[t]∈PG(3,p)} ∏_i <t,g_i>  (mod p).          (4a)

Any representative of each projective class gives the same summand. This is an interface to an integer model using projection histograms: the product is zero when the zero histogram bin is nonempty; otherwise it is the product of the nonzero field values to their multiplicities.

## 3. PROVED_HERE: integer cuts for actual bad B sequences

The following three inequalities hold:

    10 Z15(S) − 4 Z16(S) + Z17(S) ≥ 1140,               (7)
    10 Z15(S) − 3 Z17(S) ≥ 1140,                        (8)
    Z15(S) ≥ 126.                                       (9)

They rely on actual position counts and H3. They are not merely a different linear combination of the averaged congruences.

### 3.1 The 17-position inequality

For a 17-position U, any 16-zero-sum is U with one position deleted. Every such deleted position has value σ(U), the total sum of U. Therefore

    0≤Z16(U)≤3

by H3. The integer

    q(U)=Z15(U)−Z16(U)+Z17(U)

is congruent to 1 modulo 5 by (1), and is at least −3. The smallest integer congruent to 1 modulo 5 in this range is 1. Hence

    Z15(U)−Z16(U)+Z17(U)≥1.                            (10)

Sum this inequality over the 1140 subsets U of size 17 in S. A fixed 15-zero-sum is counted binom(5,2)=10 times, a 16-zero-sum is counted 4 times, and a 17-zero-sum once. This proves (7).

The multiplicity step is essential: with only multiplicity at most four, q(U)=−4 is not excluded by the congruence and nonnegativity. Thus the proof does not silently claim (10) from the older frozen multiplicity bound alone.

### 3.2 The stronger 18-position inequality

For an 18-position U let

    a=Z15(U), b=Z16(U), c=Z17(U).

Exactly as above, c≤3. Summing (10) over its 18 single deletions gives

    3a−2b+c≥18.                                        (11)

Apply (1) to U, and also sum (1) over its 17-position subsets. Reduction modulo 5 gives respectively

    a−b+c≡1,     3a−2b+c≡3,

so

    b≡2a−2,     c≡a−1  (mod5).                         (12)

For c=0,1,2,3 the least nonnegative a in its residue class is respectively 1,2,3,4. The least nonnegative b in the corresponding class is respectively 0,2,4,1. Substituting these choices, and observing that larger b can only decrease the left side of (11), gives the upper bounds 3,3,3,13 for that first possible a. All are below 18. Therefore a must increase by at least one full block of five:

    Z15(U)≥Z17(U)+6.                                    (13)

Summing over all binom(20,18)=190 such U counts each 15-zero-sum 10 times and each 17-zero-sum 3 times, proving (8).

### 3.3 Taking integer residues at the next two layers

Equation (13) in particular says every 18-position U has at least six 15-zero-sums. If V has 19 positions, counting over its 19 deletions yields

    4 Z15(V)≥19·6=114.

Thus Z15(V)≥29. The already-proved congruence Z15(V)≡1 modulo 5 sharpens this to Z15(V)≥31. Count these over all 20 deletions of S:

    5 Z15(S)≥20·31=620.

So Z15(S)≥124, and Z15(S)≡1 modulo 5 sharpens it to 126. This proves (9).

### 3.4 Checkable equality structure at 18 positions

If an 18-position U has exactly six 15-zero-sums, then (13) implies c=0. Equation (12) says b≡0 modulo 5, and (11) gives b=0. Moreover the sum of the 18 inequalities (10) is exactly 18, so each 17-position deletion contains exactly one 15-zero-sum.

Take the six 3-position complements of the six 15-zero-sums inside U. The preceding statement says each position lies in exactly one of these complements. They therefore partition U into six triples. Every triple has sum σ(U), since its complementary 15 positions sum to zero. Also σ(U)≠0: otherwise U itself would be an 18-zero-sum, forbidden by the frozen reduction to lengths at most 17.

No impossibility result for this equality structure has been established. It is a precise small configuration for a structural route to attack.

## 4. Exact scope of the positional-congruence no-go object

There is an explicit binary family satisfying all individual deletion congruences, not just their averages, as well as (5). It is deliberately a relaxation witness and is easily excluded by actual multiplicity constraints.

On 20 positions choose three 9-element sets H1,H2,H3 with pairwise intersections 0,4,4. Take every 5-subset except those contained in one of the Hi. The three removed families are disjoint, so the number of retained 5-blocks is

    binom(20,5)−3 binom(9,5)=15504−378=15126≡1 (mod25).

For d=1,2,3,4 the containment count of a fixed d-set in the full family is binom(20−d,5−d)≡1 modulo 5, while the contribution removed from any Hi containing it is binom(9−d,5−d)≡0 modulo 5. For d=0 the three removals change 4 to 1. Thus every fixed D of size at most four has containment count 1 modulo 5.

Declare the complements of these blocks to be the only nonempty zero sets; they all have length 15. Then every available individual deletion congruence for B holds. Appending a new position to each complement block, or equivalently leaving the 15-zero sets unchanged, gives the corresponding A relaxation. It also satisfies (5).

This is **not** a group sequence and does not satisfy H3 or group realizability. It does satisfy the lower cuts (7)–(9), which do not encode bounded-extension upper bounds. In fact two 15-zero sets differing only by exchanging positions i and j force g_i=g_j. The recorded family has a spanning tree of 19 such exchanges forcing all first 20 positions to have the same group value. That violates even the old multiplicity-at-most-four condition and would create a 5-zero-sum. It also violates the elementary extension bound Z15≤4 binom(20,14)/15=10336.
